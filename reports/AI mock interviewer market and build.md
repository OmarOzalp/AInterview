# Building a voice-first AI mock interviewer on APIs

**Short answer: a gap probably exists, but it is a product-quality gap, not an empty market, and the evidence for it is thin.** Candidates already pay for interview prep, from free tools up to roughly $179-225 per human mock session, and well-funded AI players exist, so demand is real. What the notes could not find is any product that combines low-latency voice, a real code editor with execution, system-design diagramming and calibrated, evidence-cited scoring at a mid price. That combination is a hypothesis drawn from competitor listings, not a finding confirmed by user research or hands-on testing. The build recommendation is firmer than the market claim: build on third-party APIs, not your own model. Use a cascaded speech-to-text, LLM, text-to-speech pipeline on an open-source orchestration framework (LiveKit Agents or Pipecat). Put a fast model on the live turns and a stronger model on asynchronous scoring. I estimate roughly **$2-4 per 45-minute session**, which supports about **$15 per session or a capped ~$29/month plan** aimed first at early-career software engineers. Every cost, margin and price figure here is my estimate on unverified, third-party inputs. Vendor sites and primary data pages were unreachable during research, many figures conflict between sources, and the section "What to verify next" lists what must be checked before committing.

## Evidence base and its limits

Almost everything below rests on web-search summaries of secondary sources: vendor-authored comparison blogs, aggregator sites, listicles and press write-ups of surveys. Primary pages (vendor pricing pages, NY Fed and BLS tables, Reddit and G2 threads) could not be opened. Where sources disagree the report says so rather than picking a number. Market-size reports are the weakest inputs: three paid-report vendors put the interview-preparation tool market at $2.8B (2025), $1.78B (2025) and $2.5B (2023), with 2030-2035 forecasts of $5-6.3B, and career coaching ranges from $1.43B to $13.6B, a spread of roughly 10x with undisclosed methodology ([Business Research Company](https://www.thebusinessresearchcompany.com/report/interview-preparation-tool-market-report), [Wise Guy](https://www.wiseguyreports.com/reports/interview-preparation-tool-market), [Verified Market Research](https://www.verifiedmarketresearch.com/product/interview-preparation-tool-market/), [Credence](https://www.credenceresearch.com/report/career-coaching-service-market), [Data Horizzon](https://datahorizzonresearch.com/career-coaching-service-market-46875)). I do not use any of them as a TAM. A credible estimate needs a bottom-up build: actively interviewing tech candidates per year, times the share who pay for prep, times spend. No verified inputs for that exist yet.

## Demand is real, but the incumbents leave a mid-priced, high-fidelity slot open

The market splits into four groups. Human marketplaces sit at the top of the price range: interviewing.io expert mocks are reported at $179 to $225+ per session depending on the source ([Lodely](https://www.lodely.com/blog/interviewing-io-pricing), [techinterview.org](https://www.techinterview.org/post/3233474681/mock-interview-platforms/)), and Exponent (which acquired Pramp in 2021) is listed anywhere from $12/month on an annual plan to $79-99/month ([IGotAnOffer](https://igotanoffer.com/en/advice/tryexponent-alternatives), [Lodely](https://www.lodely.com/blog/exponent-pricing)). Consumer AI coaches cover the middle and bottom: Google Interview Warmup is free but reportedly has a fixed question bank and no scored feedback ([GetHppy](https://gethppy.com/talent-acquisition/ai-mock-interview-tools-comparison)); Big Interview charges $39 a month and gives behavioral video feedback that is not specific to software engineering ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com.sg/software/1091862/Big-Interview)); Yoodli is a speech coach that raised a Series B reported at $40M in December 2025, though total-funding figures conflict across sources ([hyper.ai](https://hyper.ai/en/stories/a14a6cf6d55526d84408646b050ecfb6), [VCBacked](https://www.vcbacked.co/company/yoodli)). Final Round AI is the best-funded candidate-side player on the evidence found: a $6.88M seed in January 2025 and a company claim of 1M+ users, with pricing reported as high as $150/month and a review profile marred by refund and billing complaints ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/final-round-ai-job-search-journey-company-closes-6-88-million-seed/amp/), [Seedtable](https://seedtable.com/companies/final-round-ai/funding-rounds/seed-2025-01), [OphyAI, a competitor](https://ophyai.com/blog/career-advice/final-round-ai-pricing)). Its Trustpilot rating is reported inconsistently between 2.8 and 3.9 ([Weekday](https://www.weekday.works/post/final-round-ai-review)). The only free, AI-driven, software-specific option surfaced was interviewing.io's own AI Interviewer plus a few small startups such as MockInterviews.dev ([Fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-interview-prep-tools-2026/)). Paid AI prep tools typically cost about $9-30 a month, and one guide reports free tiers have shrunk over the past year ([Fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-interview-prep-tools-2026/)).

Read together, these listings leave a space between $10-30 generic AI tools and $179+ human sessions, plus a missing combination of voice, code execution and system design. That is my inference. The notes did not verify voice versus text modality for any product, did not read user reviews of the AI mocks, and did not research several named players (Apriora, Interview Kickstart, BrainRider, Huru). Hands-on testing of the top five products is the cheapest way to turn this hypothesis into a finding.

Context pushes demand up. Reported entry-level conditions are poor: new-grad hiring at large tech firms is described as under half of 2019 levels by blog and Blind sources, and NY Fed-derived coverage puts computer science unemployment around 6-7% versus about 5.6% for all recent graduates, though these come from secondary blogs and an NBER paper reportedly found no AI-linked spike ([Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/news/the-broken-ladder), [f1jobs.io](https://www.f1jobs.io/resources/blog/computer-science-unemployment-rate-2026-international-grads)). Software openings reportedly rose about 30% in 2026 even as layoffs continued, and layoff tallies conflict between trackers ([implicator.ai](https://www.implicator.ai/software-engineering-openings-jump-30-even-as-52-000-tech-workers-lose-jobs/), [Futurism](https://futurism.com/future-society/2026-tech-layoffs-surpass-2025)). Interview formats are also changing. A Pragmatic Engineer-reported survey found 81% of Big Tech interviewers suspected AI cheating and 31% said they caught it, and Google is reported to be reintroducing in-person rounds ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-146), [Storyboard18](https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-makers/google-to-reintroduce-in-person-interviews-amid-rising-ai-cheating-concerns-79624.htm)). Meanwhile employers deploy AI interviewers: a Greenhouse survey (a vendor with a commercial interest) says 63% of job seekers have faced one ([Greenhouse](https://www.greenhouse.com/newsroom/63-of-job-seekers-have-faced-an-ai-interview-most-havent-had-a-good-one-yet)), and HackerRank's Chakra is reported to have reached general availability around October 2026 after 500K+ beta interviews, which should be confirmed against HackerRank's own release ([Dealroom](https://app.dealroom.co/news/feed/hackerrank-launches-chakra-ai-interviewer-after-500k-beta-tests)). My inference is that candidates will want to rehearse against the AI-interviewer format they now face, a niche practice tools could claim. It is untested.

## Why it is not solved: hypotheses outnumber evidence

The notes support only a few barriers with sources. First, a legitimacy problem: cheating copilots such as Cluely and Interview Coder triggered employer countermeasures, and the category leader Final Round sells live-assist features, so a practice-only product has to separate itself from that brand space ([eWEEK](https://eweek.com/news/cluely-ai-cheating-app), [EveryDev](https://www.everydev.ai/developers/final-round-ai)). Second, free general assistants (ChatGPT voice, Gemini Live) are described as cheap mock-interview tools, which caps what commodity "practice questions" can charge ([Fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-interview-prep-tools-2026/)). Third, long voice sessions cost real money: third-party estimates for OpenAI realtime audio run from about $0.04-0.10 per minute to $0.30-0.45 per minute, which would put a 45-minute mock anywhere from about $2 to $20 ([Forasoft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/openai-realtime-api-pricing), [eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/gpt-realtime-mini-pricing)). The 45-minute arithmetic is mine.

Other plausible barriers have no evidence behind them: seasonal demand and high churn from a two-to-three-month usage window, acquisition cost against SEO-heavy incumbents, shallow follow-ups, voice latency and unreliable scoring of code and diagrams. The notes found no churn, retention, CAC or post-mortem data, and only one VC blog title (Malpani Ventures, "why we passed") whose content was unreadable ([Malpani Ventures](https://www.malpaniventures.com/blog/why-we-passed-an-opportunity-to-invest-in-the-mock-interview-prep-space)). Scoring reliability is the one barrier with relevant research. A July 2026 preprint auditing LLM behavioral-interview scoring reports five reliability failures, including scale collapse and test-retest inconsistency (only a snippet was readable) ([Preprints](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202607.1567)). Against that, rubric-decomposed prompting gets high but sub-human agreement with experts in related domains: RACER reports 72% agreement with two humans versus 77% human-to-human ([arXiv 2402.02656](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.02656)), and the human annotators in one interview study agreed only at alpha 0.65 ([SparkMe](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21136)). Taken together, I read the hardest unsolved problem as trustworthy scoring and realistic probing, not voice plumbing. The Malpani title is a reminder that a sharp investor looked at this category and passed, and the notes cannot say why.

## Approaches to building one, and why cascaded wins

There are two architectures. Speech-to-speech models (OpenAI gpt-realtime, Gemini Live, Nova 2 Sonic) are reported at 300-700 ms end-to-end versus 600-1,200 ms for cascaded pipelines, though another source says production cascades land at 1.5-3 s ([Coval](https://coval.ai/blog/voice-ai-models-2026/), [Gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/cascaded-voice-agent-vs-speech-to-speech-2026)). Cascaded designs keep a clean transcript, let you swap the LLM and inject rubric state, and are described as the enterprise default ([JustCall](https://justcall.io/blog/speech-to-speech-vs-cascaded-voice-agents.html)). A single March 2026 benchmark found speech-to-speech agents completing 26-38% of tasks under noise and accents versus 85% for a text-based agent, a number that needs corroboration. An interviewer needs a reliable transcript for scoring, controllable questioning and long-context handling, so cascaded is the safer default; I would revisit speech-to-speech if user testing shows the cascade feels stilted. Long sessions also expose speech-to-speech to hidden costs. One forum thread asks whether Gemini Live re-bills cumulative context each turn, and silence may be billable ([Google forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/does-gemini-live-native-audio-bill-cumulative-prompt-tokens-on-every-turn-cost-seems-to-scale-with-turn-count-not-call-duration/173248)).

For orchestration, Vapi, Retell and Bland are phone-call oriented. Vapi's $0.05 per minute headline excludes speech, LLM and voice costs and reportedly bills $0.09-0.14 all-in, while Retell's advertised range is $0.07-0.31 ([Beri](https://www.beri.net/article/voice-ai-per-minute-pricing-vapi-retell-elevenlabs-bland-cost-per-resolved-call)). LiveKit Cloud is reported at about $0.01 per agent-session minute and its Agents framework is open source ([Ecorpit](https://ecorpit.com/ai-voice-agent-platforms-vapi-retell-bland-livekit-2026/)). A browser-based interviewer with a code editor and whiteboard needs WebRTC data channels, which makes LiveKit or Pipecat the natural fit and the lowest lock-in; that is my judgment, not a sourced comparison. Component prices are reported at about $0.005-0.008 per minute for Deepgram Nova-3 streaming speech-to-text and about $0.035-0.045 per minute for Cartesia or ElevenLabs text-to-speech, with sources disagreeing on which TTS vendor is cheaper ([Costbench](https://costbench.com/compare/cartesia-vs-deepgram/), [Fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/best-ai-text-to-speech-tools-2026/)). LLM prices were not sourced in the research; the notes rely on pre-2026 background knowledge, so treat my LLM cost line as a placeholder. The code editor (Monaco or CodeMirror with a hosted sandbox such as E2B, Judge0 or Piston), whiteboard (tldraw or Excalidraw exported to a vision model) and scoring design are general-knowledge suggestions with no vendor research behind them.

## API versus your own model: use APIs now, distill only scoring later

Open components are real. Kyutai released Unmute and an open TTS in July 2025 that wrap any text LLM with open STT and TTS, though Kyutai itself notes the text "brain" loses emotion and intonation ([Kyutai](https://kyutai.org/2025/07/03/tts-unmute-open-source.html)). Moshi, a 7B full-duplex model, reports about 200 ms practical latency, but its paper's license is non-commercial and the weights' license is unverified, and a 7B model's conversational intelligence is well below frontier LLMs ([Moshi paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.00037v1)). The notes found no head-to-head evidence on technical-interview questioning or scoring for open versus frontier models. The one encouraging data point is narrow: an open Qwen3-30B judge beat GPT-4.1 at judging interview coverage in one study ([SparkMe](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21136)). Fine-tuned judges also cut the other way: they reach over 90% agreement with a GPT-4 teacher in-domain but generalize worse across rubrics and fairness tests ([JudgeLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17631), [Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02839v2)). Because rubrics change per company and role, a fine-tuned scorer would need revalidation each time.

The economics also favor APIs at launch. By my arithmetic, an always-on H100 at about $3 per hour (about $2,200 per month; GPU rental prices from aggregators) breaks even against an $0.08 per minute API near 27k minutes per month on hardware alone, and roughly 200k+ minutes per month once one loaded ML/infra engineer (assumed $15-20k per month) is counted. Those break-even figures depend on assumed concurrency and utilization that nobody measured. A pre-product-market-fit startup will not reach that volume soon, and the scarce asset for any custom model is expert-labeled scores, not GPUs. My recommendation follows the sequence the notes describe: ship on APIs with rubric-decomposed, structured scoring; log transcripts and human corrections with consent; build a double-rated gold set early; and distill the scoring and question-selection components first, since they are offline, narrow and latency-insensitive. Keep a frontier model on live turns. The "90% cost savings" from this pattern comes from an interview with OpenPipe's CEO and is not a verified case study, and no interview-prep startup was found that has publicly done it ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/kyle-corbitt-openpipe-fine-tuning-llms/)). On defensibility, raw transcripts and prompts are weak moats. Per-user longitudinal data, outcome labels (did the candidate pass real loops), expert-built company rubrics and distribution are plausible stronger ones, but this is reasoning, not evidence.

## Recommended build path, cost per session and pricing

**MVP architecture.** Use a web app with a WebRTC session on LiveKit Agents (or Pipecat). The turn loop is streaming STT (Deepgram-class), a fast low-latency LLM holding a rubric and interview-state prompt, and streaming TTS (Cartesia or ElevenLabs class). Tune endpointing for long candidate silences so the agent does not interrupt someone thinking or typing. Add an in-browser code editor whose state streams to the agent over the data channel, with execution in a hosted sandbox. Add a whiteboard only after coding works, exporting snapshots to a vision-capable model for system-design probing. After each session, run an asynchronous scoring call with a stronger LLM that returns schema-constrained scores per rubric dimension, each tied to quoted transcript evidence, then show a report with progress over time. Start with one track (coding interviews for software engineers) and defer system design, video analysis and employer features. Frame it as practice, not screening, which likely lowers hiring-law exposure (NYC Local Law 144 and the EU AI Act target employment decisions) but is a legal judgment to confirm with counsel ([Sonomos](https://sonomos.ai/blog/ai-hiring-eeoc-nyc-ll144-eu-ai-act-2026/)). Avoid face analytics until consent and biometric-privacy rules are handled.

**Cost per 45-minute session (my estimates on unverified inputs).** The cascaded design on LiveKit or Pipecat costs about $1.6-3.1 for the live pipeline (STT about $0.35, TTS about $0.5-0.8, LLM about $0.3-1.5, framework about $0.45), plus about $0.05-0.40 for post-session scoring and $0.05-0.20 for sandbox and storage, for a total of **roughly $1.7-3.7, with about $2.5-3 as a planning midpoint**. Comparators in the same arithmetic: speech-to-speech mini about $1-3, flagship speech-to-speech about $3-8 with high uncertainty, and cascaded on Vapi about $3.5-6. Payment fees add 3-5%.

**Pricing and segment.** Sell first to early-career and job-switching software engineers in an active interview loop. Price per session or in credit packs (about $15 per session, with a discount for five or ten) and a capped monthly plan around $29 for four 45-minute sessions, with transparent billing and easy cancellation, since refund disputes are Final Round's most visible complaint. This sits between the $10-30 AI tools and $179+ human mocks, above commodity voice chat, and each tier beats free ChatGPT only if the code-aware, scored session is clearly better. Avoid uncapped subscriptions: margin falls with session count, and heavy users on a flat plan are the main risk. Universities, bootcamps and employers are plausible later segments, with recurring cohorts and bigger budgets respectively, but the notes hold no pricing, sales-cycle or contract data for any of them, and employer use would add bias-audit and candidate-trust obligations (Greenhouse reports 38% of candidates walked away from an AI-interview process). Run a ten-session pilot to measure real tokens, silence share and cost before fixing prices.

## Conclusion

The sharper insight from the notes is that the question changes depending on what you test. Building the voice interviewer is the easy, well-served part: APIs and open frameworks already make a roughly $2.5 session feasible. What remains unproven is whether anyone will pay for it against free general assistants, and whether LLM scoring can be made trustworthy enough that candidates act on it. Market size, churn and acquisition cost are the least-evidenced pieces and the likeliest places for the idea to fail. The practical implication is to treat the first 10-20 hands-on sessions and a small paid pilot as the real market research, and to invest early in a double-rated gold set, because that dataset, not a custom model, is the asset that will later justify distilling the scorer.
